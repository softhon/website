Most startups don't fail because of bad code. They fail because they made the wrong architectural decisions when the codebase was small — and those decisions became load-bearing walls.

## The Premature Optimisation Trap

There's a tension every engineering team faces early: build fast and iterate, or build right and move slower. The answer isn't one or the other. It's *sequencing*.

The first version of your product should be built to **learn**, not to last. That means accepting short-term technical debt in areas where you don't yet know what the product will look like. But it also means identifying the decisions you *can't* easily reverse — and being deliberate about those from day one.

## What You Can't Easily Undo

Some architectural decisions are cheap to change. Others aren't:

- **Database schema design** — Migrations on a large table are painful. Model your core entities carefully from the start.
- **Authentication architecture** — Swapping auth providers after launch is a significant engineering project.
- **API contracts** — If you have external consumers, breaking changes are costly. Version your APIs early.
- **State management patterns** — A global state mess compounds fast. Pick a clear pattern and enforce it.

## The Rule of Three

Don't abstract until you have three concrete examples of the pattern. Premature abstraction is often worse than duplication — it creates the wrong boundary in the wrong place. Copy twice, abstract on the third occurrence.

## What Scale Actually Requires

Scale isn't about spinning up more servers. It's about:

1. **Statelessness** — Can every instance of your service handle any request?
2. **Clear data ownership** — Does each part of your system own its own data?
3. **Async where appropriate** — Are long-running jobs out of the request path?
4. **Observability** — Can you see what's happening before your users tell you?

Build these patterns in from the start — they're cheap to add early and expensive to retrofit.

## The Bottom Line

The goal isn't to build a Netflix-scale system on day one. The goal is to build a system that *can be made* to scale when you need it to — without a complete rewrite. That's a design sensibility, not an infrastructure choice.
